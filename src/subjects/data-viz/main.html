01 Chapter 1

Introduction to Data Visualization

Goals: define data visualization and express its value as a tool for understanding our world; describe the fundamental aspects of visual perception and how they relate to data visualization; learn the history of data visualization, important people in the field and contemporary practitioners; understand the preattentive attributes, the embedded meaning of colour and size, and the two thinking systems that we use to process the world around us.

1.1

What is data visualization, and why is it important?

The slides open with three signals that visual analytics matters to organisations. The first is a 2010 MIT Sloan survey. Respondents named the top three analytic techniques creating value today, and predicted which three would create the most value in 24 months. Tap a technique to follow it.

A growing field

1.2

Business intelligence: moving from data to action

Business intelligence is a path: Data → Information → Knowledge → a plan to guide actions or key decisions (Strategy). Each step answers a different kind of question. Tap a step.

Learn the four definitions word for word and the question words for each step. "How many / how much / what are" questions give you information. "Why are" gives knowledge. "How do we / what can we" leads to strategy.

1.3

How many times does the digit 7 appear?

The slides' opening exercise. Count the 7s in the plain grid with the timer running. Then switch on colour and count again. Then see the counts as a bar chart and as a pie chart. It starts with the slide's own grid (12 rows of 17 digits); "New grid" makes a fresh random one so you can try again without remembering the answer.

What the bar chart fixes

A bar chart of every digit's count lets you compare length along a common baseline, which the slides list with 2D position as the attributes for precise quantitative comparisons. Highlighting only the 7 bar answers the question at a glance.

What the pie charts get wrong (slide 24)
  • Label problems
  • Colour problems

Preattentive attributes: which one pops out?

Slide 17 shows "56789" with the 7 changed by colour, size, orientation and texture. Slide 18 repeats it with shapes: a different shape, colour, size or enclosure in a grid of dots is seen before you consciously search.

Preattentive attributes are processed by the eye in under about a quarter of a second, before conscious attention. Colour, size, orientation, shape, texture and enclosure make something "pop out". Only length/width and 2D position support precise quantitative comparison.

"Preattentive" timings (about 200–250 ms) come from vision research (Treisman, Healey). The slides show the effect but don't give a number.

1.4

A brief history of data visualization

From Ptolemy's tables to Tufte's sparklines. Tap a point on the timeline to see who did what, drawn as the chart type they gave us.

Common questions: who created the time-series line graph, bar chart and pie chart (William Playfair), who introduced the box plot and EDA (John Tukey, 1977), who wrote Semiology of Graphics (Jacques Bertin, 1967), who invented sparklines (Edward Tufte), who developed trellis charts (William Cleveland), and which map is called "the best statistical graphic ever drawn" (Minard's map of Napoleon's Russian campaign).

Slide 29 calls Playfair "founder of graphical methods of statistics" and dates him late 18th–early 19th century; slide 30 dates Minard's work 1844–1870 under "Early 19th Century", which is really mid-19th century.

1.5

Visual perception: "I see" vs "Show me"

Slides 40–68 are a run of illusions from the Brain Games video and elsewhere. Each one shows a rule your visual system follows without asking you, and each rule matters when you design a chart. Pick one and use its control.

Be ready to name the effect and give the design lesson: same colour looks different on different backgrounds (so avoid heavy background gradients); the Hermann grid and Moiré effect (so keep gridlines spaced and muted); Gestalt closure, proximity and similarity (so group related things by position and colour); context changes what we read (so label clearly).

1.6

The Ames room in 3D: why viewpoint and context matter

Slides 48–51. Through the peephole the room looks like a normal box, so the two people look like a giant and a dwarf. Drag to walk around it and the trick is revealed: the back wall slants away, so one person is much further from you. Slide 50 (a hand "pinching" a distant monument) and slide 52 (the railway tracks) use the same trick: our brain assumes a context and judges size from it.

Ames room

Peephole view: both people stand against the "back wall". The one on the right looks nearly twice as tall.

Real room: the left back corner is about twice as far from the peephole as the right back corner, and the floor and ceiling slope to hide it. The chequered floor is distorted to look square from the peephole only.

The design lesson: a chart's size judgements depend on the frame around them. 3D perspective, a non-zero baseline or an unequal axis are the chart versions of an Ames room.

1.7

Putting it all together, and "easy, right? Not so fast…"

Putting it all together
  • We live in a world that is rich with data.
  • Tools let us look at the past, present, and "try out" future scenarios.
  • Ultimately we want to understand our world and make better choices, change behaviour, grow wealth, improve quality of life, etc.
  • We discover how to do these things by asking questions through data.
  • The understanding and interpretation of data is an activity of human cognition: asking questions, discovering patterns, drawing meaning from the data; a creative, visually-driven process that also requires empirical and mathematical skills; it requires subject matter knowledge; built-in rules (or at least guidelines) affect the way we process information.
Easy, right? Not so fast…
  • Software promises to reveal the answers, but computers can't figure out what our data means, how it connects to the business or problem, and what to do.
  • Data needs are changing (e.g. big data).
  • Users expect "iPhone easy".
  • We don't train for visual intelligence.

Data + tools + brains ⋙ business intelligence

Stretching our thinking about data: most people agree a dashboard like Few's Telesales dashboard (slide 73) is data visualization. The slides then show the Cleveland Museum of Art's online collection: a grid of artwork images you can filter and open. That browsable image grid is also data visualization. Each artwork is a record, the image is how the record is drawn, and filtering it is exploring the data.

A record grid in the spirit of slides 74–77: each tile is one record (filter by type), and opening it shows the fields behind it.

Concluding thoughts
  • Ultimately we're trying to understand our world and make better choices, change behaviour, enhance wealth, or improve quality of life.
  • Learning more about the way our brains work, how we perceive and process data, and improving how we practise visualization is essential to achieving these goals.
1.8

The building blocks: an enhanced visual path, a limited working memory

We are born with an enhanced visual path to cognition
  • Approximately 70% of sense receptors are in our eyes.
  • 40% of the cerebral cortex is involved in processing visual information.
  • The visual connection to the brain has more bandwidth than other paths.
  • Visual perception is intimately connected to understanding, and this is reflected in language: "I see what you're talking about…", "Sketch out the idea…", "Seeing is believing…".
Our brain is powerful, but working memory is limited
  • Long-term memory is very important.
  • Working memory is limited to a small number of "chunks".
  • Visualization lets us consolidate complex statistics so we can process more data simultaneously (seeing the forest along with the trees).
  • The picture is not the end goal; it's what we do with it that is important.
Working memory test

The "small number of chunks" is often quoted as about 4 (Cowan, 2001), or Miller's older "7 ± 2". The slides don't give a number.

System 1 · fast

Automatic, effortless, always on: noticing the red dot, the tallest bar, a face. Preattentive attributes talk to System 1.

System 2 · slow

Deliberate, effortful, limited by working memory: counting the 7s digit by digit, reading values off a legend, doing arithmetic.

The chapter goals mention "the two thinking systems that we use to process the world", but no slide covers them. They are Daniel Kahneman's System 1 and System 2 (Thinking, Fast and Slow, 2011). A good chart hands the pattern to System 1 so System 2 can spend its effort on the meaning. Ask your lecturer how much of this is examinable.

1.9

Bertin's visual variables

According to Bertin, our perception of data on a printed page is associated with these visual variables: shape, orientation, colour, texture, value and size, plus the two planar dimensions (x and y), which are encoded in position and order. Slides 83–106 show each one as a grid of dots where one dot differs. Pick a variable, then click the odd dot as fast as you can.

The digital additions

With today's digital technology, three more factors have become critical: Motion (animated presentation of frames of data), Medium (the physical strata on which data is displayed) and Context (the sensory and emotional environment). Slide 107 asks how the video made you feel and how that differs from now: motion, medium and context influence how we perceive data and information.

Memorise Bertin's list: shape, orientation, colour (hue), texture, value, size, plus position and order on the plane. The slides split colour into hue, saturation (intensity) and value (brightness); know all three terms.

1.10

Colour: hue, saturation, value, and the colour wheel

Slide 89 separates three ways a colour can change. Move the sliders, then switch between the traditional (painters') wheel and the RGB (screen) wheel to see why the complements differ.

  • Hue: changes to the colour itself (red, orange, yellow, green, blue, purple).
  • Saturation: changes to the intensity (pale to vivid).
  • Value: changes to the brightness (light to dark).
  • Traditional colour wheel: primaries red, yellow and blue. Classical painters used this arrangement to find complements.
  • RGB colour wheel: computer displays use red, green and blue elements, which results in a shifted arrangement of complementary colours.
  • The five uses of colour (sequential, diverging, categorical, highlight, alert) come from The Big Book of Dashboards and are covered in Chapter 3. Slides 94–95 ask "Does this colour use enhance or detract?" and "What does colour even mean here?" about a rainbow-coloured Wall Street Journal chart: colour that varies without meaning detracts.

The slides spell it "compliments". The correct word is "complements" (opposite colours on the wheel).

1.11

"…and what happens now?" The 3D chart problem

The chapter ends with a 3D line chart from an old survey tool (slide 108). Perspective, tilt and depth make values hard to read and lines hide each other. Rotate this 3D chart, try to read the values, then flip to 2D.

If asked why to avoid 3D charts: the third dimension carries no data, perspective distorts length and position (the attributes we read most precisely), and front series hide back series.

1.12

Common misconceptions

MythA chart is just a prettier table.

Actually: The 7s exercise shows a chart changes what you can see. Tables are good for looking up exact values; charts let preattentive vision find patterns, outliers and comparisons, which a table can't do.

MythMore colour makes a chart easier to read.

Actually: Colour is one of the strongest preattentive attributes, so it should point at something. If every bar is a different colour, nothing stands out and the reader keeps checking the legend.

MythAll visual variables are equally precise.

Actually: Colour, shape and orientation are good for "find it", but for precise quantitative comparison the slides single out length/width and 2D position.

MythOur eyes measure colour and size objectively.

Actually: The illusions show the same grey looks different on different backgrounds and the same person looks different sizes in the Ames room. Context changes perception.

MythData visualization is a modern invention of software.

Actually: Tables go back to Ptolemy (about 150 CE), coordinates to Descartes, and the line, bar and pie chart to Playfair in the late 1700s. Software (Macintosh 1984, GUI from Xerox PARC) made it interactive.

02 Chapter 2

The Fundamentals of Data Visualization

A review of the Halloween visualization assignment (Jeffrey A. Shaffer, University of Cincinnati). Goals: learn fundamental design choices through review of the Halloween exercise; learn data visualization best practices by comparing and contrasting numerous examples; discover some of the pitfalls in the analysis of the Halloween data set.

2.1

The Halloween data set, and the trap inside it

The assignment data: trick-or-treaters counted at one house each Halloween, 2008–2016, at six times of the evening. Look closely at the numbers: they only ever go up across a row. Each column is a running total ("so far by this time"), not the number who came in that half hour. Flip the switch to derive the arrivals per half hour, and watch the chart change shape.

"Total (8:15pm)" is the year's total. Arrivals between two times = later value − earlier value, e.g. 2011, 7:00–7:30pm: 619 − 367 = 252. Several student examples on the slides plotted the running totals as if they were per-timeslot counts, which makes every evening look like it "builds up".

The slide never says the columns are cumulative; you have to notice it. The 2012 Halloween Planning Dashboard reads the per-slot counts as a "bell curve" peaking at 7:00–7:30pm, which is right for the derived counts.

2.2

Selected samples from 2012: what worked and what didn't

Examples from 2012 (slides 6–17): cumulative line charts with a 2012 forecast, small multiples, dark "Halloween" themes, and a Halloween Planning Dashboard that pairs every chart with a "Knowledge" line and a "Recommendation" line. That pairing is what makes a chart actionable (Chapter 4). Below is a rebuild of that dashboard with its claims you can test.

What the dashboard said
  • Knowledge: visits come in a bell curve, fewer at the beginning and end, more towards the middle. Recommendation: if someone offers to help, 7:00–7:30pm has historically been the busiest.
  • Knowledge: the total number of visitors has increased each year. Recommendation: purchase candy for ~1160 visitors (last year's total 869 + maximum increase 143 + a 10% buffer ~105).
  • Knowledge: with limited data, the percentage growth of the DJIA correlates with the percentage growth of visitors. Recommendation: visitor growth has almost exactly equalled 2.5× DJIA growth; with growth at 6%, prepare for 15% growth.
The pitfalls hidden in it
  • Three data points (2008–09, 09–10, 10–11) can "correlate" with almost anything. The Dow Jones has no plausible link to trick-or-treating: this is a spurious correlation.
  • The candy arithmetic doesn't add up: 869 + 143 + 105 = 1117, not ~1160.
  • "Max change = +143" isn't the maximum: the yearly totals 492, 542, 726, 869 change by +50, +184 and +143. The largest rise was +184 (2009→2010); +143 is just the latest. The "Avg. annual change = +125" is right: (869 − 492) ÷ 3 ≈ 125.7.
  • Totals don't only go up: the data from later years (2012: 673, 2013: 391) broke the "increased every year" rule straight away. A short trend is not a law.

Slide 12 says "~1160 visitors" but the parts it lists add to 1117, and its "maximum increase" of 143 is really the latest increase (the biggest was +184). This is a student sample being reviewed, so checking its numbers is part of the lesson; mention both if the example comes up.

2.3

Keep time on the x-axis, and use line charts for time series

Slides 16–21. The same five yearly totals, drawn four ways. Time read top-to-bottom (horizontal bars) or right-to-left fights the way we read time. Time left-to-right on the x-axis matches it, and a line joins the points into a trend.

The rule from the slides: keep time on the x-axis, running left to right; line charts are usually a good option for time series. Bars are fine for a few periods when each period is a separate total, but never put years down the y-axis or run them backwards.

2.4

Avoid Excel defaults: remove to improve (the data-ink ratio)

Slides 22–28 show seven students' (Persons A–G) charts built on old Excel defaults: heavy gridlines, markers on every point, a boxed legend far from its lines, borders, a grey plot area, bold titles. Then a "sigh of relief" version. Remove one thing at a time and watch the data come forward.

Remove to improve
Data-ink ratio

Data-ink ratio (Tufte): the share of a graphic's ink that shows data. Raise it by erasing non-data ink (borders, backgrounds, heavy gridlines, redundant markers) and redundant data ink, as long as nothing the reader needs is lost. "Remove to improve" (slide 28, Darkhorse Analytics) is the same idea.

The meter here is an approximation: it counts drawn elements, weighted by how much ink they use. Tufte defined the ratio conceptually; nobody measures it exactly.

2.5

Examples from 2013–2015: spot the problem, then fix it

Slides 29–46 show more student charts. Each one below is rebuilt from the slide. Decide what's wrong before you reveal it, then flip "Fix it" to see a better version.

Typical exam wording: "Critique this chart and suggest an improvement." Structure the answer as: what the chart tries to say, what gets in the way (axis, chart type, colour, clutter, misleading claim), and the fix.

2.7

Common misconceptions

MythIf two series move together, one explains the other.

Actually: The DJIA, unemployment, childhood obesity and "the Obama factor" all appear next to trick-or-treater counts on the slides. With four or five yearly points almost anything lines up. Correlation over a handful of points is not evidence of cause.

MythExcel's default chart is a safe, neutral choice.

Actually: The slides' "Avoid Excel Defaults" series shows defaults adding borders, heavy gridlines, markers and distant legends. Start from the default, then remove what doesn't carry data.

MythA line is right for any data with years in it.

Actually: A line suggests continuous change between points. Total precipitation on one night per year isn't continuous, so the slides' line through 2008–2011 invents a "trend". Dots or lollipops (slides 38–40) are honest.

MythClip art and themes make a chart engaging.

Actually: A thermometer image, an Obama photo or full-black moon pies add ink without adding data, and the photo even implies a cause. Engagement should come from a clear message.

MythThe numbers in a table mean what the column header seems to say.

Actually: The Halloween columns are running totals. Check how the data was recorded before choosing a chart, or the chart will answer the wrong question.

3.1

Colour carries a message before anyone reads a number

Same numbers, opposite feelings. Slide 4 (a "What-if" minimum-wage dashboard) uses one accent colour to separate the affected group from everyone else. Flip the framing on illustrative data.

The numbers here are made up for illustration; the slide shows the two published graphics (Simon Scarr, South China Morning Post, 2011, and Andy Cotgreave's 2014 redesign).

3.2

What meaning does colour bring to the presentation?

Slide 6 (Juice Analytics whitepaper): colour families carry moods. Apply each family to the same chart and notice how the tone changes.

3.3

The five uses of colour (The Big Book of Dashboards)

Slides 7–12. Each use answers a different question. The same Malaysian state data is drawn as a tile map and a bar chart; switch the colour use and read what each one tells you.

  • Sequential: colour is ordered from low to high (e.g. unemployment rate by state). One hue, light to dark.
  • Diverging: two sequential colours with a neutral midpoint (e.g. profit by state, losses one colour and gains another, zero neutral).
  • Categorical: contrasting colours for individual comparison (e.g. quantity by category and sub-category).
  • Highlight: colour used to highlight something (one line in colour, the rest grey).
  • Alert: colour used to get the reader's attention (one red dot on the one late inspection).

Know one example of each from the slides: sequential = unemployment rate by state map; diverging = profit by state map; categorical = quantity by category and subcategory bars; highlight = one state's sales line in blue among grey lines; alert = the red dot next to "19 days" since last inspection.

Slides 13–14. Short-term memory holds only "small chunks of information", so many colours force you to reuse the same or similar colours and to keep referring to the legend. Add categories and watch the legend take over, then switch to highlight.

3.5

Colour vision deficiency (colour blindness)

The eye with normal colour vision

Three types of colour-sensitive cones:

  1. Short (S): respond to short wavelengths.
  2. Medium (M): respond to medium wavelengths, more sensitive to green colours.
  3. Long (L): respond to long wavelengths, more sensitive to red colours.
Types and how common (per 100 men)

Together about 8% of men have colour vision deficiency. Dichromats (2 working cones) see no perceptible difference between red, orange, brown and green.

Simulate it. Pick a chart and a type of colour vision. The simulation runs on the chart itself, so it also works on charts you build in the SQL lab.

Normal vision
Simulated

Simulation uses the Machado, Oliveira and Fernandes (2009) model at full severity. The slides used VisCheck (www.vischeck.com) and Color Oracle-style images; results look similar. Tritanopia is rare and isn't on the slide's list.

3.6

Designing colourblind-friendly visualizations

Slides 22–32. Traffic-light colours (red, yellow, green) collapse to olive and yellow for deuteranopes. The fixes on the slides: use blue and orange instead of red and green, or keep the traffic-light idea but change the lightness so red is dark, yellow is light and green is mid. Test your own palette here.

  • Traffic light palette: #E22049 #FFF200 #0D9E49. Under deuteranopia red and green both become olive.
  • Alternate traffic light palette (slide 31): #8E191C #FFDE17 #83C775, and (slide 32): #8D1D1C #FFDE17 #83C79B. Dark red vs light green stay apart because their lightness differs.
  • Colourblind-friendly blue and orange (slide 24): the bars stay distinguishable under protanopia.
  • In scatter plots with three categories (Furniture, Office Supplies, Technology), orange and green dots merge under protanopia; region colours (Central, East, South, West) merge under deuteranopia.
  • Slide 33's joke "Reasons why I hate pie charts: because I am colour blind" shows a pie with every slice the same grey.

Three-part answer for "make this colourblind friendly": avoid red/green pairs (use blue/orange); vary lightness, not only hue; and don't rely on colour alone (add labels, position, shape or direct annotation).

The distinguishability score is the CIE76 colour difference (ΔE) between the simulated colours. Below about 20 two swatches are easy to confuse on a chart; above about 40 they're clearly different. These cut-offs are rules of thumb.

3.7

Common misconceptions

MythColour blind people see in black and white.

Actually: Most have one weak or missing cone type (protan, deutan). They see colour but confuse certain hues, especially red, orange, brown and green. True greyscale vision is rare.

MythA rainbow palette is good for ordered data.

Actually: Rainbow hues have no natural order and change lightness unevenly. Ordered data needs a sequential palette (one hue, light to dark) or diverging if there is a meaningful midpoint.

MythEvery category deserves its own colour.

Actually: Beyond a handful of colours the reader can't remember them and keeps checking the legend. Highlight what matters and grey the rest, or split into small multiples.

MythRed and green are fine because everyone knows "red = bad, green = good".

Actually: About 8% of men can't tell them apart. Use blue/orange, change lightness, or add icons and labels.

MythColour is just decoration you add at the end.

Actually: The framing example shows colour (and direction) sets the emotional message. Choose colour on purpose: sequential, diverging, categorical, highlight or alert.

04 Chapter 4

Exploratory vs. Explanatory and Dashboard Design

Goals: learn exploratory vs. explanatory visualizations; discuss the definition of a dashboard; learn fundamentals of dashboard design; learn what makes data visualization actionable.

4.1

Process of discovery: exploratory vs. directed

"No matter how well-designed your graphic is, users will want to know 'why' or to look at things in a different way." The same store data, two ways. Exploratory gives you the tools; explanatory (directed) shows the answer to a pre-known question.

Exploratory discovery
  • Give users tools to find the answer.
  • Allows analysts and power users to find new patterns and relationships.
Directed discovery
  • Show the answer to a pre-known question.
  • Options to change basic parameters.
  • Suitable for general consumption by a wide variety of users.

Dashboards are a form of directed discovery. A "faceted analytical display" is closer to exploratory: several interactive views of one dataset for analysis.

4.2

What is a dashboard?

A dashboard is a visual display of the most important information needed to achieve one or more objectives; consolidated and arranged on a single screen so the information can be monitored at a glance.

Stephen Few (2004), "Dashboard Confusion", Intelligent Enterprise, 20 March 2004

A dashboard is a visual display of data used to monitor conditions and/or facilitate understanding.

The Big Book of Dashboards (2017)

Another way to think about it: an information display designed for people to help maintain situational awareness.

The Big Book of Dashboards

vs. a "faceted analytical display": a set of interactive charts (primarily graphs and tables) that simultaneously reside on a single screen, each of which presents a somewhat different view of a common dataset and is used to analyze that information.

Stephen Few (2007), "Dashboard Confusion Revisited", PerceptualEdge.com, March 2007
Dashboards: supporting attributes (Few 2013, Information Dashboard Design, p. 28)
  • Information is presented using small, concise, direct and clear display media: clearly stated messages; each point limited to the space needed.
  • Customized: tailored to the needs of a specific group or individual.
  • Consistent layout: data changes over time, but the interface is consistent (until it's time for the next revision).

Be able to quote Few's 2004 definition and point to its key phrases (most important information, one or more objectives, single screen, at a glance), then contrast it with the broader 2017 Big Book definition (monitor conditions and/or facilitate understanding).

4.3

Dashboard and KPI rollups: different views for levels of the organization

Slides 12–13. Each level sees a different scope, fed by the same operational data sources. Tap a level.

4.4

Compare and contrast: avoid useless bling

Slides 15–21. A "blinged" oil production dashboard (3D bars, gradients, green background) next to the "unblinged" version; a balanced scorecard of eight gauges (low effectiveness) next to Few's CIO dashboard (high effectiveness). Same KPI, three displays: compare how much room each needs.

Avoid useless bling (slide 18)

Some widgets and displays provided by tool vendors are glitzy but unhelpful, like gauges. They:

  • require a disproportionate amount of room for the information presented,
  • are hard to read and understand,
  • provide little actionable information.

On the CIO dashboard slide: "One gauge takes the same space that can accommodate twenty data points in this dashboard design."

Bullet graphs and sparklines are Few's replacements for gauges: a bullet graph shows actual, target and qualitative ranges in a thin bar; a sparkline shows the recent trend in a word-sized line.

4.5

Dashboard "dos", layout basics and eye scanning

Dashboard "dos" (Few 2013, p. 28)
  • Content position and size should match its importance and frequency of use.
  • Use colour and formatting to draw attention where needed, rather than to decorate.
  • Visually associate data and content that is related.
  • Use the needs of the user to drive the layout, rather than forcing layout with an inflexible grid (note: this is a consideration when choosing tools).

When deciding placement, consider how the eye will scan the page.

Eye-tracking heat maps of web pages show the well-known "F" pattern: attention falls heavily on the top left and fades down and to the right. The slides warn it's partly a product of text-heavy pages; patterns change with content and with the medium (a newspaper is scanned differently). For dashboards the slides give a simple guide: top-left most emphasis, top-right and bottom-left neutral, bottom-right least. Place the four tiles, then check the heat map.

When asked where to put the most important KPI: top left (most emphasis), with supporting detail in the neutral quadrants and the least critical content bottom right. Mention that dominance of the neutral quadrants can change with content.

4.7

Key performance indicators (KPIs) and the bullet graph

KPI

A KPI is a type of performance measurement, used to evaluate success or achievement against a specific goal.

  • Can be operational or strategic.
  • Helps to identify areas that need attention: more "where to look" vs. "this happened".
  • Knowledge of "what matters" to an organization makes KPIs relevant.
  • Often included in dashboard displays.

You must know what you're measuring and what performance is desired before you create a KPI.

4.8

The Duell rules for actionable visualizations

Created by Eric Duell, used by permission on the slides. Step through the five rules; each one updates the mini dashboard on the right, built in the style of the slides' complaints and inspections examples.

    Learn the five rules in order: (1) the question to answer must be identifiable; (2) the data needed must be available; (3) the visualization should be tailored to the person who will use the information; (4) the story uncovered in the visualization should be evident; (5) the action required should be clear. Rule 1 is different from exploratory data analysis, where you don't start with a fixed question.

    4.9

    Common misconceptions

    MythAny page with several charts is a dashboard.

    Actually: By Few's definition a dashboard shows the most important information for specific objectives, on a single screen, to be monitored at a glance. A set of interactive charts for analysis is a "faceted analytical display".

    MythGauges look like a car dashboard, so they're ideal.

    Actually: Gauges take a lot of space for one number and are hard to compare. A bullet graph or sparkline shows the same value, its target and its trend in a fraction of the space.

    MythOne dashboard can serve the CEO and the team equally.

    Actually: KPI rollups give each level its own view: strategic for the CEO, tactical for managers, operational for teams. Duell rule 3 says tailor it to the person.

    MythA KPI is any number you track.

    Actually: A KPI evaluates success against a specific goal. Without a target and a sense of what "good" looks like, it's just a metric.

    MythPeople always read a dashboard in an F pattern.

    Actually: The F pattern comes from text-heavy web pages. The slides stress that content and medium change scanning; big numbers, colour and dark backgrounds pull the eye too.
